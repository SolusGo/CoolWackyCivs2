# The Grand Registry

The Grand Registry is the institution through which the Boundless City remembers what it owes. Its principal halls stand near the First Stone, but its working life extends through ward houses, reach offices, traveling clerks and the households that preserve receipts. The phrase names both a building and a network of responsibility.

## A memory distributed among the wards

Each ward keeps the records needed for ordinary life: household standing, local works, leases, reserves and the decisions affecting them. Reach offices reconcile matters crossing ward boundaries. The principal archive preserves common appropriations, final judgments, official standards and the history of the city as a continuing undertaking.

A change travels with evidence. A household entering a new ward brings testimony of its previous standing; a completed work brings measurements and the acknowledgment of those expected to use it. Conflicting copies are preserved while the difference is examined. An office that silently removes an inconvenient earlier entry deprives the city of the means to discover its own error.

The Great Register Fire transformed these practices from uneven customs into a celebrated public discipline. Registrar Ilen Vale's reforms demanded separated copies and witnessed corrections after burned central books left thousands of ordinary claims exposed. The archive's recovery depended on evidence held outside it.

Every later medium changes the work. Shelves, printed forms, mechanical indexes and more rapid communication increase the amount the city can remember. They also increase the importance of knowing which records are authoritative and how an ordinary person can challenge a mistake replicated across the whole network.

## What the books contain

A Registry entry records a relationship the city may need to act upon. A farm's boundary matters alongside the water it may draw, the path others use through it and the obligations attached to its tenure. A public granary's capacity matters alongside its actual contents and the households relying on them.

The working books consequently connect land to people and commitments. They distinguish a proposed district from an inhabited one, a promise of wages from a payment, and the declaration of a road from evidence that travelers can use it. Skilled clerks learn to record uncertainty instead of disguising it beneath a precise-looking number.

Public accounts can be inspected. Household information receives protection proportionate to its purpose. A citizen's right to prove their standing does not give every official a claim to every detail of their domestic life. The balance between useful knowledge and intrusive administration is argued in the Registry's halls as intensely as in the Council chamber.

The institution's authority comes from faithful recordkeeping. The Common Bench gives judgments; the Council grants appropriations; the Registry preserves their terms and exposes the difference between what was authorized and what occurred. A clerk can identify a missing signature and must never confuse that discovery with a power to decide the entire dispute.

## Stone, memory and civic culture

Visitors first notice the public colonnade, where measures, completed undertakings and obligations still outstanding are displayed. Behind it lie rooms devoted to the less spectacular work of comparison: a new plan against an older boundary, a contractor's receipt against a ward's account, an official's summary against the testimony of residents.

The Registry acts as a monument because the city chooses to remember its common undertaking there. School groups visit the First Books and study corrections in their margins. Guilds preserve examples of workmanship and the names of crews whose work survives. Frontier reports bring distant districts into the city's shared account of itself.

That cultural role can become an instrument of power. Official histories can praise a monument while omitting the people removed for it, or describe a contested claim as a grateful neighborhood's welcome. The most respected keepers preserve evidence that complicates the story their own institution would prefer to tell.

Above the public copying tables stands the Registry's demanding motto: 'Publish the accounts. Protect the person.' Its two instructions keep the archive from measuring success solely by the number of entries it holds. A city remembers well when its records help the people within them obtain what is due.
